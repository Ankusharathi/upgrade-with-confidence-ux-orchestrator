# The Decision Trail: A Connected UX Orchestrator Case Study

## Evidence notice

This is a synthetic academic demonstration. It uses an invented telecom transaction, fictional products and prices, a local prototype, public-source evidence, and evaluator-led workflow checks. It contains no Verizon customer research, production analytics, measured conversion improvement, or accessibility conformance claim.

## From a request to a defensible decision

AI can generate UX deliverables quickly. Speed becomes a problem when the reasoning disappears: assumptions begin to resemble findings, artifacts stop connecting to decisions, and polished screens arrive without a visible record of why they should exist.

I created the UX Orchestrator to preserve that record. I then tested it by taking one focused upgrade question through Intake, Discover, Define, Design, Validate, and Deliver:

> “Before I place this order, what exactly changes?”

The result is not only a future-state interface. It is a visible trail from the initial request to the final design response, including the evidence boundaries, human approvals, failed observations, revisions, and unresolved risks.

## The operating contract

Every phase uses the same contract:

`Inputs → Specialist agent → Skills → Artifacts → Human gate → Next phase or revision`

The Orchestrator manages state and routing. Specialist agents perform bounded work. Skills define reusable methods. Artifacts preserve evidence and decisions. Gates stop the project until an accountable human decides whether the work can advance.

The system is intentionally not an autonomous designer. Its value is that AI assistance becomes inspectable.

## The upgrade decision

A device upgrade can combine consequences that occur on different timelines: tax, a device payment, a one-time fee, a plan effect, shipping, trade-in value, and promotional credits that may begin later or depend on conditions.

Public evidence supports that transaction complexity. It does not prove that customers are confused, that they abandon because of it, or that a redesigned summary would increase conversion. Those remain hypotheses for future research.

The project therefore focused on one design outcome: make the complete upgrade impact understandable before commitment without presenting estimates as guarantees.

## What each phase changed

### Intake — define the boundary

Intake turned a broad upgrades idea into one bounded pre-commitment decision. It established the eligibility-to-confirmation scope, the human owner, the responsive web surface, and the rule that synthetic work could not become a real-customer claim.

### Discover — separate evidence from suspicion

Discover used public sources and a current-experience audit to identify the variables the design needed to represent. Transaction complexity became evidence. Confusion, abandonment, and conversion lift remained hypotheses.

### Define — choose the decision to improve

Define narrowed several possible problems to one outcome: help someone distinguish what is immediate, recurring, delayed, estimated, or conditional before placing an order.

### Design — create the explanation

Design introduced a stable decision aid called **Upgrade Impact**. It begins empty, grows only from confirmed choices, labels uncertainty, and remains editable until commitment.

The six-step flow separates eligibility, configuration, trade-in, impact review, final review, and confirmation. It provides correction paths without losing context and prevents an unknown submission result from becoming an unsafe duplicate retry.

### Validate — let the prototype challenge the promise

The first evaluator-led dry run exposed two contradictions:

1. The initial screen displayed configured financial values before the related choices existed.
2. Failure recovery encouraged another action before order status was known.

The project returned to Design. The revised prototype now begins with **Not calculated** and reveals values only after their causes are confirmed. The unknown-order branch preserves the configuration, checks status first, and enables one safe retry only after confirming that no order exists.

The failed observations remain in the record. The retest is recorded separately rather than replacing them.

### Deliver — preserve the full trail

Deliver connects the framework, phase records, current-state reference, visual audit, proposed design, interactive prototype, validation changes, Upgrade Guide, and evidence limitations in one final-submission experience.

## Current, audit, and proposed design

The supplied reference establishes a recognizable upgrade journey across line selection, device shopping, configuration, and confirmation. The audit identifies observable design gaps without presenting them as usability findings:

- financial effects appear at different moments;
- trade-in value, timing, and conditions are not part of one connected decision;
- the clearest summary arrives after commitment;
- correction and consequential recovery are not demonstrated in the connected flow.

The proposed design responds with one persistent Upgrade Impact view. It groups due-today cost, next-bill effect, ongoing monthly impact, trade-in status, credit timing, plan effect, shipping, and one-time fees. Every material choice remains editable before the single commitment action.

## Upgrade Guide’s role

Upgrade Guide is a separate public-information proof of concept grounded in a verified 150-question Verizon FAQ set. It explains general topics such as upgrade eligibility, trade-in requirements, plans, activation, and device selection.

It is not part of the synthetic Northstar transaction and has no access to a customer account, order, bill, private eligibility status, live inventory, or guaranteed offer. When an answer depends on private data, the assistant stops and directs the user to account-aware support rather than guessing.

This separation is intentional: the transaction manages confirmed choices, while the assistant handles general questions that do not belong inside the checkout.

## What the work demonstrates

The project demonstrates:

- a six-phase governed UX process;
- visible evidence, assumption, artifact, and approval boundaries;
- traceability from a problem to a design response and validation criterion;
- an executable prototype with correction and recovery behavior;
- a revision loop that preserves failure rather than hiding it;
- a grounded FAQ assistant with an explicit account boundary.

It does not demonstrate real customer comprehension, conversion improvement, production feasibility, accessibility conformance, or live-system integration.

## Closing reflection

The Orchestrator did not remove uncertainty. It made uncertainty inspectable.

The most important outcome is not a single screen. It is the record of how the work changed: a broad request became a bounded decision, evidence remained separate from hypothesis, the prototype was allowed to contradict the strategy, and a human remained responsible for every gate.

The future-state design is now ready for the next legitimate step—research with real participants—not for an unsupported claim of success.
